# PEPTIDES AND HEALTHY AGING: SEPARATING RESEARCH FROM HYPE

If you've searched for "anti-aging peptides", you've met a wall of confident claims: younger cells, longer telomeres, more energy, better skin. Some of the science behind those claims is genuinely interesting. Very little of it has been tested properly in people.

This guide grades the most talked-about names honestly, using a simple scale, so you can tell the difference between "promising in a lab" and "proven in humans".

> **KEY TAKEAWAYS**
> - No peptide is authorized in Canada or the US as a treatment for aging.
> - Most of the evidence behind popular "aging" peptides comes from animal and cell studies, or from very small human studies.
> - Some cosmetic peptide ingredients have small human studies on skin appearance. They are cosmetic ingredients, not medicines.
> - Health Canada's April 2026 warning named several injectable products marketed for aging, including NAD+, GHK-Cu, MOTS-c, Epitalon and SS-31.
> - The habits with the strongest evidence for healthy aging are still the unglamorous ones: movement, sleep, not smoking and regular care from your own health professional.

## HOW WE GRADE THE EVIDENCE

We use five plain grades. They describe the *type* of evidence, not whether something works for you.

- **GRADE A:** Authorized medicine, with large human trials, for a specific condition.
- **GRADE B:** Approved only abroad, or only for a narrow or rare condition.
- **GRADE C:** Small human studies (a few dozen people or fewer, often without a placebo group).
- **GRADE D:** Animal and cell studies only.
- **GRADE E:** Anecdote, influencers and seller blogs.

Remember: an animal result is a reason to keep studying, not a reason to conclude anything about people.

## NAD+ (NOT A PEPTIDE, BUT ALWAYS IN THE CONVERSATION)

**What it is:** NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is a coenzyme found in every cell, involved in energy metabolism and many basic cell processes. It's often sold beside peptides, but it isn't one.

**What the research covers:** Most human research looks at oral "precursors", such as nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), which the body can convert into NAD+. Reviews published in 2023 to 2025 agree on two things. First, these precursors reliably raise NAD+ levels measured in blood. Second, effects on things people actually care about, such as physical function, metabolism or blood vessels, have been mixed and often null. One recent review also noted that the idea of NAD+ falling with age has been consistently measured in only a limited number of human studies.

**Injectable and IV NAD+:** The human evidence is limited to small pilot studies. Health Canada named NAD+ in its April 2026 warning about unauthorized injectable products.

**Grade: C** for oral precursors raising a blood marker; **C to D** for any broader claim.

## GHK-CU (COPPER PEPTIDE)

**What it is:** A naturally occurring three-amino-acid peptide that binds copper. It's been used in cosmetics for decades.

**What the research covers:** Laboratory studies are extensive and interesting. Human studies are small. A 2025 review in *BioImpacts* pointed to a surprising absence of clinical studies of topical GHK-Cu, and a 2024 review called published information on its skin penetration and effectiveness insufficient. The human data that exist are small, short studies.

**Injectable GHK-Cu** is a different matter entirely. It isn't authorized as a medicine in Canada, and Health Canada named GHK-Cu in its April 2026 warning.

**Grade: C** as a cosmetic ingredient on the skin's surface; **D** for anything injected.

## OTHER SKINCARE PEPTIDES

Signal peptides such as palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 (sold under trade names like Matrixyl) are among the better-studied cosmetic peptides. A 2017 review in the journal *Cosmetics* describes a 93-person, randomized, placebo-controlled, split-face study that measured wrinkle appearance over several weeks. That's a real study, but it's one study, of a cosmetic outcome, funded within the cosmetics field. [VERIFY study details against the original paper]

In Canada, products like these are regulated as cosmetics, which means they can describe appearance ("the look of smoother skin") but can't legally claim to change how the body works.

**Grade: C** for skin appearance in short studies.

## MOTS-C

**What it is:** A small peptide encoded in the DNA of mitochondria, the cell's energy-making structures. Scientists discovered it in 2015. [VERIFY year]

**What the research covers:** Reviews in *Diabetes & Metabolism Journal* (2022) and *Frontiers in Endocrinology* (2023) describe how exercise raises the body's own MOTS-c levels in muscle and blood. Most of the metabolic findings, though, come from mice. A company tested a modified version (called CB4211) in one small early human trial. As of 2026, published reviews and evidence trackers report no completed human efficacy trials of MOTS-c itself.

There's an important distinction here: your body making more MOTS-c when you exercise isn't the same as injecting a lab-made version. There is no human evidence that the second reproduces the first.

**Status:** Not authorized in Canada; named in Health Canada's April 2026 warning; prohibited in sport by the World Anti-Doping Agency.

**Grade: D,** with one small early human study of a related compound.

## EPITALON

**What it is:** A lab-made four-amino-acid peptide (sometimes spelled epithalon), developed by a research group in Russia led by Vladimir Khavinson.

**What the research covers:** Much of the excitement comes from cell studies reporting increased telomerase activity, an enzyme involved in maintaining the protective ends of chromosomes. Reviewers point out three issues. Most of the research comes from one laboratory group; much of the supportive human data involves a related older extract rather than epitalon itself; and no placebo-controlled human trial has measured telomere length before and after epitalon. Reviewers also note an open safety question, because telomerase activity is also a feature of many cancers.

**Status:** Not authorized in Canada; named in Health Canada's April 2026 warning.

**Grade: D,** with small, older human reports.

## SS-31 (ELAMIPRETIDE)

**What it is:** A lab-made peptide made to target mitochondria.

**Where it stands:** In September 2025, the US FDA granted accelerated approval to elamipretide (brand name Forzinity) for Barth syndrome, a rare inherited disease. That approval is narrow: one rare condition, under conditions of continued study. It isn't an approval for aging, energy or general wellness, and it doesn't apply in Canada. Health Canada named SS-31 in its April 2026 warning about unauthorized injectable products.

**Grade: B** for Barth syndrome in the US only; **D** for anything else.

## WHY THE HYPE OUTRUNS THE SCIENCE

Aging research is genuinely exciting, and mouse studies can be striking. But mice live about two years, are genetically similar and are studied in controlled labs. People are not. Many promising results never survive the move to large human trials. A McMaster University analysis published in 2026 summed up the wider peptide market neatly: the hype is outrunning the evidence.

A few questions help cut through:
- **In whom?** Mice, cells or people?
- **How many?** Twelve people, or twelve thousand?
- **Compared with what?** Was there a placebo group?
- **Who says so?** An independent journal, or a seller's blog?

## WHAT DOES HAVE STRONG EVIDENCE

The least exciting answer still has the strongest evidence. Canada's 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for adults recommend regular physical activity, including muscle-strengthening activity, plus good sleep and less sedentary time. Not smoking, limiting alcohol and keeping up with regular check-ups and screening with your own health professional remain the foundations of healthy aging.

None of that fits in a vial. All of it is backed by far more evidence than anything above.

## FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

### ARE THERE ANY PEPTIDES APPROVED FOR AGING?
No. No peptide is authorized in Canada or the US as a treatment for aging. Some peptides are approved medicines for specific diseases, and some peptide ingredients are used in cosmetics.

### DOES NAD+ SLOW AGING?
That hasn't been established in people. Oral NAD+ precursors raise NAD+ in the blood, but human studies of real-world outcomes have been mixed and often show no effect. NAD+ is a coenzyme, not a peptide.

### DOES EPITALON LENGTHEN TELOMERES IN HUMANS?
That hasn't been shown. The telomerase findings come mainly from cell studies, and no placebo-controlled human trial has measured telomere length before and after epitalon.

### ARE COPPER PEPTIDES IN SKINCARE WORTH IT?
Copper peptides are established cosmetic ingredients with small human studies on skin appearance. Reviews note that larger, well-designed clinical studies are still lacking. Injectable copper peptides are not authorized in Canada.

### CAN MOTS-C REPLACE EXERCISE?
No. Exercise raises the body's own MOTS-c, but no completed human trial has shown that an injected, lab-made version has the effects of exercise.
