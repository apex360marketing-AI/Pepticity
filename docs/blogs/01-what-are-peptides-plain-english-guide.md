# PEPTIDES, EXPLAINED IN PLAIN ENGLISH

If you've scrolled social media in 2026, you've probably seen the word "peptides" more than once. Some posts make them sound like a breakthrough. Others make them sound dangerous. The truth is calmer and more interesting than either.

This guide covers what a peptide actually is, how peptides are made, the main families you'll hear about, why they're everywhere right now, and how strong the evidence really is. No hype, no jargon you can't look up, and no promises.

> **KEY TAKEAWAYS**
> - A peptide is a short chain of amino acids. Your body makes thousands of them, and some of the best-known medicines in the world, such as insulin and semaglutide, are peptides.
> - "Peptide" is a chemistry word, not a safety word. Being "natural" says nothing about whether a lab-made product is pure, legal or right for you.
> - For most peptides sold online, the evidence is early: mostly animal and cell studies.
> - In April 2026, Health Canada warned Canadians to think twice before injecting peptides bought online, and it has since gone to court against a seller.
> - The honest question to ask about any peptide is: what kind of evidence exists, in whom, and who is accountable for the product?

## WHAT IS A PEPTIDE?

Amino acids are the building blocks of protein. Link two or more together and you have a peptide. Link roughly 50 or more together and scientists usually call it a protein. The line is blurry: insulin, with 51 amino acids, sits right on it.

Your body runs on peptides. Oxytocin, insulin and GLP-1 are all peptides your body makes to send signals from one place to another. Glutathione, one of the body's main antioxidants, is a tiny peptide of just three amino acids.

A **therapeutic peptide** is a lab-made copy of one of these natural signals, or a modified version of one (scientists call that an *analogue*). Analogues are usually built to last longer in the body or to act on a chosen target more precisely.

One practical point explains a lot about this category: most peptides are broken down in the gut, like the protein in food. That is why many peptide medicines are given by injection rather than as a pill, although there are exceptions.

## HOW ARE PEPTIDES MADE?

There are two main ways.

### CHEMICAL SYNTHESIS

Most short peptides are built one amino acid at a time on tiny resin beads, a method called solid-phase peptide synthesis. Its inventor, Bruce Merrifield, received the 1984 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. After the chain is built, it is cut free, purified (often by a technique called HPLC) and usually freeze-dried into a powder.

### BIOLOGICAL PRODUCTION

Longer peptides and proteins, such as human insulin, are often made by engineered cells, a process known as recombinant DNA technology.

Either way, the hard part isn't making *a* peptide. It is making the *right* peptide, at the stated amount, without harmful leftovers or contamination, batch after batch. That is exactly what drug regulators check before a medicine is authorized, and exactly what an unregulated product skips.

## THE MAIN FAMILIES YOU'LL HEAR ABOUT

You don't need to memorize these. The point is that "peptides" covers very different things with very different levels of evidence.

| FAMILY | IN PLAIN WORDS | WHERE THE EVIDENCE STANDS |
|---|---|---|
| Incretin medicines (GLP-1 and related) | Lab-made versions of gut hormones involved in blood-sugar regulation | Large human trials; several are Health Canada-authorized prescription medicines |
| Insulin and other hormone medicines | Copies of the body's own hormones | Decades of clinical use as prescription medicines |
| Growth-hormone signalling peptides | Copies of the brain signals that tell the pituitary gland to release growth hormone | One is approved abroad for a narrow use; most sold online are not authorized in Canada |
| Body-protein fragments | Lab-made pieces of larger proteins found in the body | Mostly animal and cell studies; tiny human pilots at best |
| Melanocortins | Act on receptors involved in skin colour and other signals | One is a US-approved medicine for a narrow use; others carry safety warnings |
| Copper peptides | A three-amino-acid peptide carrying copper, long used in cosmetics | Small cosmetic studies on the skin's surface |
| Mitochondrial peptides | Peptides that act on, or come from, the cell's energy-making machinery | Mostly animal data; one is approved in the US for a single rare disease |
| Bioregulators | Very short peptides from a Russian research school | Mostly cell and animal work, largely from one group |

And a note on what *isn't* a peptide: NAD+, often sold beside peptides, is a coenzyme, not a peptide at all.

## WHY ARE PEPTIDES TRENDING IN 2026?

A few things happened at once.

**The GLP-1 effect.** Peptide medicines like semaglutide became some of the most talked-about drugs on the planet. In 2026, Canada became the first G7 country to authorize a generic version of semaglutide. That success made the word "peptide" sound powerful, and other products borrowed the shine by association.

**Social media and influencers.** Short videos travel faster than clinical trials. A September 2026 report on rising use of the so-called "Wolverine" peptide blend described use climbing despite a lack of human testing.

**Policy noise in the US.** The US Food and Drug Administration moved several peptides between its compounding lists in 2026, and an FDA advisory committee met on peptides in July 2026. Headlines about those changes were often read, wrongly, as "approval". No final decision on those peptides had been announced at the time of writing.

**Easy online access.** Websites selling peptides labelled "for research use only" multiplied. Regulators in both countries have since said that label doesn't make human sale legal.

## WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE REALLY SAY?

Here is a simple ladder for judging any peptide claim, from strongest to weakest:

1. **Authorized medicine with large human trials.** The approval covers a specific product, for a specific use, in specific people. It doesn't transfer to a copy, a "research" vial or a different goal.
2. **Approved only abroad, or only for a rare condition.**
3. **Investigational,** with human trials under way but no approval yet.
4. **Small human studies only.** A handful of people, often without a placebo group.
5. **Animal and cell studies only.** Useful for scientists; not proof that anything works or is safe in people.
6. **Anecdotes, forums and seller blogs.** Not evidence.

Most peptides being marketed online in Canada sit on rungs 4 to 6. In a 2026 analysis for McMaster University, kinesiology professor Stuart Phillips described one widely promoted peptide's research base as dominated by rodent and cell studies, with only a few very small human reports, and argued that the hype has outrun the evidence. He also made a fair counterpoint: peptides aren't implausible as medicines, and some of the most successful drugs of the decade are peptides. The question is always *which* peptide, and *what* evidence.

Two habits help when you read a claim:
- **Ask "in whom?"** A result in mice, or in a lab dish, is a starting point, not an answer.
- **Remember failed trials count too.** Several peptides that were heavily marketed online have human trials that did not meet their main goal.

## WHAT HEALTH CANADA HAS SAID

In April 2026, Health Canada issued an advisory titled "Think twice before injecting peptides bought online", warning that unauthorized products can seriously harm you. It named products including BPC-157, CJC-1295, Epitalon, GHK-Cu, ipamorelin, KPV, Melanotan I and II, MOTS-c, NAD+, SS-31, TB-500 and retatrutide, and said many others are sold too. News coverage of the advisory listed possible risks such as hormonal imbalance, blood-sugar problems, liver or kidney damage and blood clots.

Health Canada also made an important distinction: authorized injectable peptides, such as insulin and GLP-1 medicines, exist and are regulated as prescription drugs. The issue isn't peptides as a class. It's unauthorized products of unknown quality.

Enforcement has moved beyond warnings. In June 2026, a Québec court granted Health Canada a permanent injunction stopping a company called Canlab Research from selling unauthorized injectable peptides in Canada.

## HOW TO THINK ABOUT PEPTIDES, SENSIBLY

- **Separate the word from the product.** Insulin is a peptide. So is a powder of unknown purity in an unlabelled vial. Same word, completely different risk.
- **Check the legal status.** In Canada, an authorized drug has an eight-digit Drug Identification Number (DIN) you can look up in Health Canada's Drug Product Database.
- **Be wary of sellers who give medical instructions.** A seller is not your health professional.
- **Talk to a licensed health professional** you trust about anything you're considering for your own body, especially if you're pregnant, have a medical condition or take other medicines.

## FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

### WHAT IS THE SIMPLEST DEFINITION OF A PEPTIDE?
A peptide is a short chain of amino acids, the building blocks of protein. Chains of roughly 2 to 50 amino acids are usually called peptides; longer chains are called proteins.

### ARE PEPTIDES LEGAL IN CANADA?
It depends on the product. Some peptide medicines, such as insulin and GLP-1 medicines, are authorized prescription drugs. Health Canada says injectable peptides are regulated as prescription drugs and must be authorized before they can be sold, and it has warned that many peptides sold online are unauthorized.

### ARE PEPTIDES SAFE?
There's no single answer, because "peptide" covers many different substances. Authorized medicines have been reviewed for safety, quality and effectiveness for specific uses. Unauthorized products haven't, and Health Canada warns they may be contaminated, mislabelled or harmful.

### IS NAD+ A PEPTIDE?
No. NAD+ is a coenzyme, a helper molecule involved in energy metabolism. It is often sold alongside peptides, which causes confusion.

### HOW CAN I TELL IF A PEPTIDE PRODUCT IS AUTHORIZED IN CANADA?
Look for a Drug Identification Number (DIN) on the label and search it in Health Canada's Drug Product Database. A drug sold in Canada without a DIN is generally not authorized, with limited exceptions.
