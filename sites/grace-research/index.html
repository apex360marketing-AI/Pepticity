WHERE THE LINE IS (read this first)

The short version: we sell the store, the brand and the experience. We never say what a product does to a body.

  • Why. In Canada, a product that has no Health Canada approval can't be sold or advertised as something that treats, fixes or improves a health problem. Most of this catalogue has no approval.
  • Enforcement is real. Health Canada named most of these peptides in a 2026 public warning. In June 2026 a Québec court shut down another online peptide seller (Canlab) for good.
  • "For research use only" does not protect us. Health Canada says so. In August 2026 the US FDA sent warning letters to five "research" sellers. It used their dose charts, mixing instructions and bacteriostatic water sold beside the peptides as proof the products were really meant for people.
  • So the interest cards say what area a product class has been studied in. Example: "researched in the context of cellular energy". That describes published science. It does not promise the visitor anything.
  • We never say: treats, cures, fixes, boosts, heals, burns, builds, reverses, "helps with", "for pain", "for headaches", "for weight loss". No condition names next to a product.
  • No dosing, ever. No amounts, no schedules, no "cycles", no mixing steps, no syringes, no calculators. That's the exact thing the FDA letters used.
  • No sexual-function anything. PT-141 gets no interest card and no "what it's for" line. Same for anything about libido or intimacy.
  • No medical talk. No doctor, clinician, pharmacy, prescription or consult wording (owner rule) until those partners really exist.
  • Bacteriostatic water never sits beside the peptides. Not on a shelf, not in a bundle, not as "you may also need".
  • The biggest risk is listing the products at all, not the wording. Counsel must clear the catalogue before the store goes public. Until then the store runs in preview.
  • The 15% offer, member prices, restock alerts on peptides and referral credit all sit behind one switch (COUNSEL_CLEARED.offer). When it's off, the same spots show the member card, Grace's letter or a news list.
  • Every number is real. Stock counts, queue positions, reviews and countdowns come from real data or don't show. Fake urgency gets fined (Competition Bureau; one retailer paid $3.25M).
  • Every email and text needs a ticked box the visitor ticked themselves (CASL). One box per purpose. Never pre-ticked.
  • US visitors: same rules plus FDA, FTC, CAN-SPAM and TCPA (texts). HGH in the US is a federal crime risk outside a doctor's order. Recommendation: serve Canada only until counsel says otherwise.

8. TOP 15 RECOMMENDATIONS (ranked by sales impact)

  1. Hero offer card first. Grand opening, 15%, one email field, GUEST or JOIN. The first screen decides most visits.
  2. All-in price in the cart drawer. Shipping shown before checkout. Surprise costs are the #1 reason carts are dropped (39–48%, Baymard via secondary).

  5. Interest entry cards with one-field capture. Turns "I need energy" into a shelf and a sign-up, with no claims.
  6. Restock queue with a real position and an instant email. Back-in-stock is the best-converting automation in the research (6.7–8.3%).
  7. Welcome series that delivers the 15% with a real end date. The code arrives in the inbox, not just on screen.
  8. 3-tap catalogue finder with SAVE THIS SHORTLIST. Helps the unsure buyer; quiz tools claim 7–25% (vendor, test it).
  9. Quantity breaks instead of goal bundles. "3 OF THE SAME VIAL · SAVE [X]%". Raises order size without pairing products with a goal (counsel switch).
  10. Free-shipping line in the drawer, A/B tested. One test showed order size +9.2% but conversion −5.9%. Set a real threshold [$X] and keep a hold-out.
  11. Lot passport, the day Grace has real certificates. Trust matters most on expensive items. Never before it's real.
  12. Verified reviews after five. Five reviews vs none lifted purchase likelihood ~270% (Spiegel 2017). No health outcomes in reviews.
  13. Refer a friend from the thank-you page and the dashboard. Milestone programmes drove ~30% of new subscribers for Morning Brew (secondary).
  14. Welcome back + tab-title cue. Returning visitors land on their saved cart instead of starting over.
  15. Thank-you page text opt-in. Shipping updates (asked-for) plus a separate unticked "new lots" box: the highest-trust moment for a second consent.

Ten upgrades for the client portal and the owner dashboard, ranked by impact. Researched 2026-10-29. Not legal advice.

Read first - House rule: product name, price and quantity only. No health claims, no dosing, no "cycle", no "research use only". - Most stats are vendor figures: a direction, not a promise. - Counsel must still confirm PEPTICITY may sell Health Canada-unauthorized peptides at all.

1. LOT & LAB LIBRARY

  • Client sees: every bottle in "My orders" carries its lot number. One tap opens that lot's third-party lab report (PDF) plus test date and lab name.
  • Why: ConsumerLab says a lab report only means something if it comes from an independent lab and matches the same lot (ConsumerLab). Thrive (Canada) links each lot number to its report (Thrive).
  • Hook: "YOUR BOTTLE. YOUR LOT. YOUR REPORT."
  • Effort: M (owner uploads a PDF per lot; orders are tagged with the lot).
  • Compliance: show only the lab's own facts: identity, purity %, lot, date, lab. No "pharmaceutical grade", no "safe", no "clinically pure". Never show a report that does not match the lot.

2. AUTO-SHIP WITH SKIP & DELAY

  • Client sees: "Deliver every [4/6/8/12] weeks". The next charge date and amount sit beside one-tap SKIP, DELAY 2 WEEKS, SWAP and CANCEL.
  • Why: vendors say skip and pause options cut voluntary churn by roughly 11-25%, and Skio reports 14% (Skio, Rework). Recharge reports a 9.6% save rate on cancel attempts (Recharge).
  • Hook: "NEVER RUN OUT. NEVER GET STUCK."
  • Effort: M (needs a subscription billing tool).
  • Compliance: the customer picks the schedule. We never suggest a schedule based on use ("lasts 30 days" is a dosing hint). Auto-renewal terms beside the button, easy online cancel. Do not sell bacteriostatic water as an add-on.

3. MEMBER TIERS (STATUS, NOT DISCOUNTS)

  • Client sees: the member card moves from MEMBER to RESERVE to PRIVATE as the customer spends. A progress bar shows what the next tier unlocks: first access, free express shipping, a direct line to the owner.
  • Why: Accenture finds members bring 12-18% more revenue growth a year. LoyaltyLion says members are 47% more likely to buy a second time (LoyaltyLion). Sephora's Rouge tier works on status, not price (Open Loyalty).
  • Hook: "RESERVE MEMBERS SEE IT FIRST."
  • Effort: L.
  • Compliance: reward with service perks, not points per milligram. If counsel classes any product as prescription, provincial pharmacy rules may ban loyalty inducements on it.

4. LIVE ORDER JOURNEY

  • Client sees: a branded timeline (PACKED, SEALED, SHIPPED, OUT FOR DELIVERY, DELIVERED) with a photo of the sealed parcel and live carrier status.
  • Why: vendors report 43-72% fewer "where is my order?" messages and higher repeat buying from branded tracking pages (EcommerceFastlane, LateShipment).
  • Hook: "WATCH IT LEAVE OUR HANDS."
  • Effort: S-M (adds to the existing tracking).
  • Compliance: low risk. Keep the packaging discreet and leave product names off the outside of the box.

5. ONE-TAP REORDER + MY REMINDER DATE

  • Client sees: "REORDER LAST BASKET" in one tap, plus a reminder date the customer sets ("Remind me on…") by email or text.
  • Why: replenishment emails convert about 8-28% (Website Magazine / Listrak).
  • Hook: "YOUR USUAL, ONE TAP AWAY."
  • Effort: S.
  • Compliance: the date is the customer's own. Never work out a "you'll run out on…" date from quantity, because that implies a dose. CASL: texts need separate express consent.

6. PRIVATE DROPS & FIRST ACCESS

  • Client sees: a "RESERVED FOR YOU" window when stock comes back. Members get 48 hours of first access before the public, and waitlist members get first place in line.
  • Why: Sephora gives its top tiers first access to big sell-outs (Antavo).
  • Hook: "BACK IN STOCK. YOU'RE FIRST."
  • Effort: M.
  • Compliance: no fake scarcity. Countdowns and "only X left" must be true (Competition Act). Never sell a member-only item publicly later.

7. WALLET MEMBER CARD

  • Client sees: "ADD TO APPLE WALLET / GOOGLE WALLET". The pass shows the tier, member number and referral code, and updates live with restock and tier alerts.
  • Why: vendors report wallet alert open rates of about 60-80%, against about 20% for email (Issuepass).
  • Hook: "YOUR CARD, ALWAYS ON YOU."
  • Effort: M.
  • Compliance: brand name and tier only, with no product names on the pass (it shows on the lock screen). Treat alerts as marketing messages under CASL.

8. REFERRAL STANDINGS

  • Client sees: a private progress ladder ("3 friends joined, 2 more unlocks PRIVATE"), with credits earned and pending. There is no public leaderboard.
  • Why: a peer-reviewed Wharton study found referred customers are worth at least 16% more and stay longer (Schmitt, Skiera, Van den Bulte 2011).
  • Hook: "BRING TWO. MOVE UP."
  • Effort: S (builds on the existing give 15% / get 15%).
  • Compliance: the customer shares their own link. We do not send emails to friends on their behalf (CASL). Public rankings are out, because they expose who buys peptides.

9. SURPRISE & DELIGHT DESK (OWNER SIDE)

  • Client sees: at random, a handwritten card, a free express upgrade or an anniversary note from the owner, never promised ahead.
  • Why: surprises are remembered more than rewards customers expect. Rotate them so they never become an expectation (Wiser Review). There is no hard study, so measure gifted against not-gifted customers ourselves.
  • Hook (owner): "5 CUSTOMERS TO SURPRISE THIS WEEK" list, with one-click send.
  • Effort: S.
  • Compliance: never gift a peptide sample (it counts as advertising and supply). Gifts are shipping, service, packaging and stationery.

10. TRUST & PRIVACY CENTRE

  • Client sees: one page covering what we store, data download and delete, discreet packaging, the refund and damage policy in plain words, and "you are talking to the owner, not a bot".
  • Why: health-adjacent shoppers worry most about privacy.
  • Hook: "YOUR DATA. YOUR CALL."
  • Effort: S.
  • Compliance: PIPEDA and Québec Law 25 (access, deletion, a named privacy officer). No order data sent to ad pixels.

SKIPPED ON PURPOSE (TOO RISKY)

  • Education library / protocols / calculators: FDA's August 2026 letters used exactly these to show intended human use.
  • Community forum and reviews: customers will post results and doses, and those become our health claims.
  • "Usage tracker" or "days left" counters: these imply a dose.

TOP 3 RECOMMENDATION

  1. LOT & LAB LIBRARY: the trust moat. Grey-market sellers cannot copy it honestly.
  2. AUTO-SHIP WITH SKIP & DELAY: the biggest lever for repeat revenue, with the customer in control.
  3. MEMBER TIERS: turns the existing member card into status, and powers drops, the wallet card and referrals later.

1. EMAIL AND SMS CAPTURE OFFERS

1. WELCOME OFFER IN EXCHANGE FOR EMAIL

  • What: A popup or inline form that gives something of value for signing up.
  • Real examples: Research-peptide shops commonly use first-order codes, for example Elite Peptides at 10% for newsletter signup (SimplyCodes) and Nox Peptides (Vancouver) at 20% for first-time customers (press release). AG1 instead gives a "welcome kit" of gifts with the first subscription, such as travel packs and vitamin D3+K2; reported contents vary by source (Garage Gym Reviews) [UNVERIFIED on AG1's own site].
  • Why it converts: Klaviyo puts standard homepage pop-up forms at 3 to 8% (vendor figures) (Klaviyo). Popupsmart's median is 0.83% of displays, with the top 10% at 3.87% or more (Popupsmart data).
  • PEPTICITY version: Make the welcome gift knowledge, not a discount: "THE PEPTICITY PRIMER, free, plus GRACE'S LETTER monthly" (see #8 and #16). A money offer may only apply to things outside the Rx and pharmacy lane, such as [cosmetic topicals, if sold], branded merch, or an event ticket.
  • Risk: STOP for any discount, gift or "welcome kit" tied to an Rx, a peptide program or the pharmacy fill (provincial inducement bans; C.01.044). Whether a discount on the clinician consult fee is allowed is a counsel question (provincial colleges of physicians may have their own inducement rules) [UNVERIFIED]. A welcome discount on unauthorized peptides is illegal.

2. FREE, DISCREET DELIVERY AS THE STANDARD (NOT A THRESHOLD)

  • What: Free shipping, either always or above a threshold, used as an incentive.
  • Real example: Felix (Canada) advertises free shipping on all medication (Expert Beacon review; Lifetimes Canada). A Canadian research shop advertises free shipping over $150 CAD (Peptide Protocol Wiki).
  • Why it converts: Unexpected extra costs are the top cart-abandonment reason in Baymard's research. It is reported as 39% or 48%, depending on the base, via secondary sources (Contentsquare summary).
  • PEPTICITY version: Put "Discreet delivery included" on the all-in price card (#20). Never use a spend threshold on Rx. Use a threshold only on non-Rx shop items.
  • Risk: CAUTION. Free delivery of an Rx is part of the pharmacy service and is normal practice (Felix). But "free shipping if you spend $X on prescriptions" looks like an inducement. Keep it flat and unconditional.

3. PROVINCE WAITLIST ("NOT IN YOUR PROVINCE YET")

  • What: Visitors outside a served area join a list to hear when service opens there.
  • Real examples: Function Health ran a waitlist from its April 2023 beta. The company reported nearly 100K signups by January 2024, and "skip the line" codes were used (Fitt Insider Q&A; Wikipedia). Superpower had about 150,000 people waitlisted at public launch (TechCrunch).
  • Why it converts: A waitlist turns a dead end (wrong province) into a lead. The scarcity is real because licensure really is provincial.
  • PEPTICITY version: When the quiz's province question is unserved, show "WE'RE NOT LICENSED IN [PROVINCE] YET". Ask for email and province only. Use an unticked "Also send me GRACE'S LETTER" box, kept separate from the "tell me when you open" consent.
  • Risk: SAFE. Never publish waitlist counts unless they are real and current. Don't promise opening dates.

4. FOUNDING LIST / EARLY ACCESS

  • What: A pre-launch list that gets first booking access.
  • Real example: Next Health offered a limited number of "Founding Memberships" with lifetime discounts at new clinics (Business Wire, Green Hills).
  • Why it converts: It gives status and first access, and it is the classiest form of urgency when the limit is real.
  • PEPTICITY version: "THE FOUNDING LIST": first access to consult booking when a province or program opens, a founder's welcome letter, and an invitation to the first live session (#13). Any cap must be real, for example "[N] consult slots in the first week", tied to actual clinician hours.
  • Risk: CAUTION. "Lifetime discounts" on Rx or pharmacy services are STOP (inducement). Perks must be access and information, not price on Rx. If there's a stated cap or deadline, honour it (Competition Bureau).

5. "NOTIFY ME" ALERTS (RESTOCK / OPENINGS)

  • What: One-tap alerts when something comes back or opens.
  • Real example and data: Across 3,900+ stores, the top quartile of back-in-stock alerts converts above 15.7% (vendor data) (notify-me.io benchmark). Omnisend data reported back-in-stock as the highest click-to-conversion automated email at 7.3% (EcommerceDB).
  • PEPTICITY version: Use it for consult openings ("Tell me when an evening slot opens with a clinician licensed in [province]"), new guide releases, and live-session dates.
  • Risk: STOP for "drop alerts" on Rx drugs or peptides: that is product promotion, and for unauthorized peptides it is promoting an illegal sale. Alerts about service capacity are SAFE.

6. SMS OPT-IN (TWO-TAP)

  • What: On mobile, a button opens the visitor's messaging app with a pre-filled text. They tap Send and are subscribed.
  • Real example and data: This is Attentive's Two-Tap. An agency reports a 2 to 3x lift over standard SMS forms (anecdotal) (ATTN Agency). Klaviyo cites 10 to 13% for phone-only capture forms (vendor) (Klaviyo).
  • PEPTICITY version: Keep two separate lanes. The first is transactional texts: appointment reminders and "your clinician's note is ready". The second is marketing texts, opt-in only and rare (a monthly "letter is out", or a new province open).
  • Risk: CAUTION. CASL covers SMS, so you need identification, STOP handling and a consent log. Never put health details in an SMS (lock screens are visible). A transactional opt-in is not marketing consent.

7. EXIT-INTENT OR TIMED SLIDE-IN (OFFERING THE GUIDE)

  • What: A panel that appears when the cursor leaves the page, or after time or scroll depth.
  • Data: Klaviyo reports exit-intent forms at 10 to 13% (vendor) (Klaviyo). Popupsmart's larger dataset found the exit-intent advantage not reliable (1.10% vs 0.78% median, possibly chance). Conversion falls from the third view, and by the sixth view it is 31% of the first-view rate (Popupsmart, exit intent; Popupsmart data).
  • PEPTICITY version: One quiet frosted-glass slide-in, bottom corner on desktop and a bottom sheet on mobile. Show it once per session and at most three times ever. Offer the PRIMER (#8) or "Email me this page". Never show it over the quiz or the booking flow.
  • Risk: SAFE if it's dismissable and offers no Rx discount.

8. QUIET ANNOUNCEMENT BAR

  • What: A slim top bar with one current message and one link.
  • Example: Best-practice guidance says banners should sit at the top, be dismissable and be time-limited (popups skill guide, Corey Haines) [weak source; general practice].
  • PEPTICITY version: Rotate true service news: "NOW BOOKING CLINICIAN CONSULTS IN [PROVINCES]", "THE OCTOBER LETTER IS OUT: READ IT", or "LIVE Q&A [DATE]: SAVE A SEAT".
  • Risk: SAFE. No timers, and no drug names.

9. THE FREE PRIMER (GUIDE OR EBOOK)

  • What: A downloadable guide given for an email address.
  • Real examples: Peter Attia gives a five-tactic "Longevity Toolkit" on newsletter signup (peterattiamd.com/newsletter). Huberman Lab's free newsletter gives new subscribers a digest with a "Complete Sleep Toolkit", a fitness protocol and a cold-exposure guide.   • Real example: Attia's free "Longevity 101" is eight lessons in eight days, each under 15 minutes (peterattiamd.com/longevity-101-guide).

0. THE FINDINGS THAT MATTER MOST

  1. Most of the non-GLP-1 catalogue is named in Health Canada advisories as unauthorized. Health Canada's public advisories on seized and online-sold injectable peptides name BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, ipamorelin, Epitalon, GHK-Cu, NAD+, AOD-9604, DSIP, MOTS-c, retatrutide and others. Health Canada says a "research use only" label does not make a product legal. Selling unauthorized drugs is illegal in Canada. This is a counsel question before it is a copy question (see 7.1).
  2. In Canada, a consumer-facing ad for a prescription drug may state only the name, the price and the quantity (Food and Drug Regulations C.01.044). The quiz result page therefore should not put "your goal" next to a named Rx drug. The thing being sold should be the clinician consult and the care service. Advertising a service is outside the Food and Drugs Act, but naming products for an indication is not.
  3. Regulators target "sameness" and "clinically proven". The US FDA sent warning letters in September 2025, March 2026 (30 letters) and June 2026 (25 letters). They targeted telehealth claims that compounded GLP-1s are "the same active ingredient as Ozempic", are "clinically proven", or come from an "FDA-approved pharmacy". Health Canada authorized generic semaglutide in April to June 2026, so an approved option now exists in Canada.
  4. Showing the result before asking for email is the right default for this category. Vendor data says gating the result before email captures more emails. But the health-information consent rules (PIPEDA: express consent for sensitive data) and CASL (no pre-ticked boxes) favour showing the result first, then asking with an unticked opt-in. That matches the current build.
  5. The "not a doctor" message works best as several quiet layers. These are a subtitle in the bot header, one line in the first bot message, one line under the quiz result, a required consent line at checkout, and the footer. Research reported by MIT Technology Review found medical disclaimers in AI health answers fell from 26% (2022) to 1% (2025), and researchers link the missing disclaimers to over-trust. Under California AB 489 (in force 2026-01-01), an AI tool may not imply that a licensed human is giving the advice. So the bot must never be named or styled like a clinician.

Labor illusion People valued a travel search more when it showed the work being done ("checking airline X") than with a plain progress bar, even with waits up to about 60 s. Buell & Norton 2011, Management Science 57(9). A blind progress bar did not help. The "building your plan" moment should name the real steps it is doing ("Matching your focus… Checking your province… Preparing your next steps"). Keep it to about 2 to 3 s and don't fake a medical analysis.
Multi-step forms Often claimed lifts ("up to 300%", "86%") have no traceable primary data [UNVERIFIED]. One reported case went from 0.96% to 8.1% after switching the same fields to multi-step [secondary]. Sources agree multi-step helps mainly on long forms. One question per screen is right because the quiz has more than 5 inputs.
Form completion Zuko (2025, via CrazyEgg): 45% of form visitors and 66% of form starters complete. Typeform has reported a 47.3% completion rate (2023). No source gives a completion-versus-question-count curve. Measure drop-off per question (Typeform's method: stops at Q ÷ views of Q) rather than guessing an ideal length.
Quiz benchmarks ConvertFlow, Sept 2026, 1,063 lead-gen quiz campaigns: lead captured from 7.85% of visitors, 23.84% engage. Product quizzes (2,120 campaigns) engage 44.2%. Octane AI (vendor) claims 7 to 25% quiz-to-purchase versus 2 to 3% general ecommerce, and a 60% "industry average" completion. These are vendor-selected figures. Use as rough targets only. Track them yourself.
Email gate timing Vendor figures (Octane AI): gating before the result gives 45 to 55% opt-in, after the result 25 to 35%, and an optional skip 35 to 42%. Methodology isn't shown. See 1.3: for health data, the result comes first.
Checkout friction Baymard (via secondary blogs): about 70% average cart abandonment. Top reasons are extra costs (about 39%), forced account creation (about 19 to 24%) and a too-long checkout (about 18%). Edition and year are unclear [secondary]. Show all-in pricing on the result page. Use guest booking with no forced account before the consult.

1.3 Lessons for the build

  • Keep the quiz short: 10 to 12 taps, about 2 minutes. Noom proves long can work when every screen feels personal, but Noom sells a habit app. PEPTICITY's intake belongs to the clinician, so the quiz only needs to route. The clinician's own intake can go deeper after booking, the way Function Health and Ro do it.
  • Ask easy questions first and sensitive ones last, but always before the result. Every documented flow does this.
  • Show the result before asking for an email. Gating captures more emails in vendor data, but PEPTICITY collects health answers. PIPEDA treats health-related data as sensitive, needing express consent. CASL rejects pre-ticked boxes. A result held hostage behind an email also reads as "salesy", which works against the classy tone. Offer "Email me this summary" as an optional extra on the result page, with an unticked marketing box.
  • Sell the next step, not the molecule. Ro, Juniper and Hims Canada all end on "a licensed provider reviews". Juniper doesn't name the drug at all. That is also the lawful Canadian ceiling (see 3).
  • Say up front what isn't charged if they're declined. Ro does this. The FTC's NextMed order and Canada's drip-pricing rule (Cineplex, $38.9M penalty) both punish hidden costs.

Allowed: guidance, education, navigation Not allowed: medical advice

4.2 Emergency handling (deterministic, before any AI reply)

Run a keyword and intent check before the vetted answers or the AI: chest pain, can't breathe, fainting, severe allergic reaction or swelling, severe abdominal pain, vomiting that won't stop, thoughts of self-harm. When it matches, reply with only this, and no product talk:

"That sounds urgent. Please call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department now. If you're thinking about suicide or self-harm, call or text 9-8-8 (free, 24/7, across Canada)."

The 9-8-8 line launched nationwide on 2023-11-30, by call or text, in English and French (PHAC). Ada's "roadblock" pattern is the reference: it stops the flow and doesn't continue the sale. Add a "Talk to a person" button that shows [phone number] and "Call me back".

4.3 Handing off to a human or clinician

  • Any question about a person's own body, condition, medications or dose gets one warm sentence, then a next step: "Book a consult", "Ask our team" or "Call me back". Never a dead end.
  • Pass the context (quiz category, flags) to the human so the visitor doesn't repeat themselves. Store it only with consent.
  • Log refusals to tune the vetted-answers set. No health text goes into ad or analytics tools (see the PIPEDA remarketing rule above).
